# Beyond Studying
Now that we have covered actionable techniques, it is time to examine the factors that influence our learning.

## Physical Factors
### Sleep
Sleep is crucial, as it is highly correlated with attention, executive function, and memory, as well as physical performance; it is also where memory consolidation takes place. You can check how much sleep you need based on your age at this link: [click here](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/public-education/brain-basics/brain-basics-understanding-sleep#Heading2). For better sleep quality, the basics include:

1. Going to sleep at the same time every day
2. Waking up without an alarm (if possible)
3. Getting sunlight exposure shortly after waking up
4. Sleeping in complete silence and darkness
5. Reducing light exposure before sleep [^38]

### Exercise
A systematic review found that aerobic physical activity improved academic performance and cognition [^39], and another systematic review found similar results where academic performance and cognition were linked to physical activity [^40]. Aerobic exercise, HIIT, combined training, and resistance training increase BDNF levels in both healthy and non-healthy individuals [^41], which is relevant for synaptic plasticity and retention [^42]. Following exercise, BDNF levels increase and return to baseline after 1 hour [^43], and exercising 4 hours after learning has been shown to improve retention [^44].

### Hydration
Dehydration of as little as 1% of body weight degrades cognitive abilities, but calculating exact water intake manually can be difficult. Here is a link discussing this: [click here](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/how-much-water-should-you-drink). The recommended way to stay hydrated is drinking water gradually throughout the day, avoiding chugging, and not waiting until you feel thirsty. [^45] [^46]

## Other Factors
### Pomodoro
The Pomodoro technique consists of studying in study-rest blocks, typically 25-5 (minutes), helping reduce mental fatigue and procrastination. Ideally, use a 25-5 format for tasks you tend to procrastinate on and 50-10 for tasks requiring deep focus. It is important to avoid cognitively demanding tasks during breaks and to combine breaks with wakeful rest. [^47]

### Workspace
A study found that poor ergonomics significantly affect students psychologically, causing fatigue, anxiety, reduced concentration, emotional burnout, and lower academic performance [^48]. Another study demonstrated that workspace layout does not influence individual perception of creativity across different generations (Baby Boomers to Millennials) [^49], and a lack of exposure to natural light in the workspace is associated with elevated cortisol levels and lower melatonin levels at night, which are linked to depressive symptoms and poorer sleep quality [^50].

### Screen Time
In a study where participants were blocked from mobile internet access for two weeks and restricted to non-mobile internet access (via desktop computers), participants improved their focus capacity by 91%, alongside better mental health and well-being. This may be explained by increased real-life engagement, more sleep, improved social connections, and reduced multimedia consumption [^51].

### Artificial Intelligence
A study found that higher trust in artificial intelligence is associated with lower critical thinking, whereas higher self-confidence is associated with greater critical thinking:
> "When using GenAI tools, the effort invested in critical thinking  
> shifts from information gathering to information verification; from  
> problem-solving to AI response integration; and from task execu-
> tion to task stewardship."[^52] 

Another study found a significant negative correlation between frequent use of AI tools and critical thinking skills; furthermore, a higher academic level was associated with better critical thinking skills regardless of AI usage. [^53]

### Google Effect
It is the tendency to forget information that can easily be found online using a search engine, driven by the belief that it can be looked up whenever needed. In one study, participants exhibiting the Google effect showed poorer overall cognitive performance and lower emotional intelligence [^54]. Another study found that the effect only manifested for professionally non-specific information compared to professionally specific information, which can be explained within the framework of responsible remembering [^55]. The effect is independent of gender, age, and measurement method, though it has been shown to be less prevalent in North America [^56].

## Mindbuilding
Mindbuilding, or mental exercise, is the practice of exercising your mind to build mental fitness. It refers to several scientifically backed mind workouts that make you smarter, less distracted, and less impulsive. Mental fitness includes mental ability: memory, processing speed, reaction time, reasoning, problem-solving, and reading speed and comprehension. Mental fitness includes concentration, focus, attention, and other executive functions. Mental fitness also includes abilities involved in mental control, such as self-control and emotional control. Mental exercise makes you better at thinking and learning. You learn to focus and pay attention for longer. You get distracted less often. You get better at controlling your emotions and actions. You become less impulsive. That's what it means to build mental fitness.

### Relational Reasoning Training
Relational reasoning training is a type of cognitive training based on Relational Frame Theory—a theory stating that language and cognitive abilities are grounded in the capacity to relate stimuli in an arbitrary and derived manner. These tasks are based on 5 relational frames: distinction, comparison, spatial, temporal, and hierarchical [^57]. Multiple studies have shown it can increase IQ and transfer to academic performance [^58]; however, other studies have failed to replicate these results [^59], which may be attributed to methodological limitations and small sample sizes [^60].
