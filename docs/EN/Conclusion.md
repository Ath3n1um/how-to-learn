# Conclusion

## Which Techniques Should You Use?

Now that we’ve looked at the techniques and factors that help us learn better, it’s time to wrap up with a summary of which techniques to use and how to apply them. To do this, we’ll classify them into three categories based on the requirements of the material you’re studying: universal techniques, which are always used; techniques geared toward theoretical knowledge; and techniques aimed at improving problem-solving skills.

|Requires|Techniques|
|---|---|
|Universal|Elaborative Interrogation  <br>Self-explanation  <br>Spaced repetition  <br>Pomodoro  <br>Wakeful rest|
|Theory|Cornell Notes  <br>Questionable Methods:  <br>- Highlighting <br>- Rereading|
|Problem-Solving|Interleaved Practice  <br>Worked Examples|

## How to Apply These Techniques

- Study in blocks of 25 minutes of study followed by a 5-minute break for activities you tend to procrastinate on, and 50 minutes of study followed by a 10-minute break for tasks that require deep concentration; incorporate wakeful rest during the break.
- Try to learn from materials that include step-by-step solved examples with explanations, and alternate between different topics or types of problems during a study session.
- When studying, ask yourself and answer questions like “Why?” and explain to yourself what you’re learning.
- When taking notes, divide your page into a left section for keywords, a right section for your notes, and a bottom section for a summary of your notes.
- Review your notes at gradually longer intervals.
